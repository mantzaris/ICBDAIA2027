III From-Signals-to-Evidence Framework Conventional underwater acoustic systems compress an observation into a decision, ,whether a detection flag or a class label, discarding the intermediate physical reasoning that a human expert would articulate. The proposed framework in contrast makes the intermediate reasoning explicit and organizes the entire processing pipeline around an ordered feature–evidence–decision chain, as illustrated in Fig. 2 ℱ = ϕ ⁡ ( 𝒳 ) , ℰ = g ⁡ ( ℱ , 𝒫 ) , 𝒟 = h ⁡ ( ℰ ) , \mathcal{F}=\phi(\mathcal{X}),\qquad\mathcal{E}=g(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{P}),\qquad\mathcal{D}=h(\mathcal{E}), (1) where 𝒳 \mathcal{X} denotes the multimodal observations, 𝒫 \mathcal{P} the textual physical priors, ϕ \phi the acoustic encoder, and g g , h h the evidence-forming and decision-making processes realized by the shared reasoning core. Raw observations are first transformed into physically meaningful representations ( feature ); the system then identifies and states the acoustic structures that bear on the problem at hand, in light of available physical priors ( evidence ); final judgments are produced conditioned on the stated evidence ( decision ). Notably, the decision 𝒟 \mathcal{D} is conditioned on the evidence ℰ \mathcal{E} rather than directly on the features ℱ \mathcal{F} , which distinguishes this chain from post-hoc explanation schemes in which justifications are generated independently of the decision process. Two further principles guide the design. First, physical grounding is maintained throughout the processing chain. Extracted features correspond to identifiable acoustic structures, such as tonal lines, envelope modulations, and machinery-related components, rather than opaque activations, allowing the resulting evidence to be verified against the input. Second, heterogeneous information is represented within a shared semantic space. Acoustic signals, time–frequency representations, and textual physical priors are mapped into the embedding space of a large language model (LLM), which serves as the unified reasoning core. Although the framework is instantiated differently for detection and recognition, both tasks follow the same feature–evidence–decision chain and share the same reasoning core. III-A Multimodal Input Layer The framework is designed to accommodate the heterogeneous information available in underwater sensing, without prescribing a closed set of input types. Representative inputs include, but are not limited to: (i) waveform-level observations , i.e., the received time series from single or multiple hydrophones, possibly preprocessed by a physical front end such as beamforming; (ii) time–frequency imagery , including general-purpose spectrograms and physics-informed representations such as time-varying line spectra and DEMON spectra, which render specific acoustic structures visually explicit; (iii) environmental and geographic information , such as sound speed profiles, bathymetric charts, sea state, and seabed properties, expressed either as imagery or as structured parameters; and (iv) textual physical priors and expert knowledge , expressed in natural language. Whereas conventional architectures can absorb such heterogeneous information only through ad-hoc feature engineering, the language-modeling interface allows imagery, parameters, and textual priors to be aligned into a unified semantic space and injected as context or instructions, without architectural modification. The input layer is thus extensible: new information sources can be incorporated by adding the corresponding encoders or simply by extending the textual context. III-B Physics-Aware Acoustic Encoding The acoustic encoder ϕ \phi in Eq. ( 1 ) maps observations to physically meaningful tokens, each associated with an identifiable acoustic structure, providing concrete referents for subsequent evidence statements. Its design follows three principles. First, complementary time–frequency representations are encoded through separate pathways rather than fused at an early stage, preserving the distinct physical characteristics of each representation. Second, the encoder is trained on physics-oriented analysis tasks, such as shaft-frequency and modulation analysis for recognition and coherent integration and bearing analysis for detection, rather than optimized solely for the final decision. This encourages the learned tokens to capture how a target sounds, rather than merely which label it belongs to. Third, conventional physical processing, including filtering, normalization, beamforming, and Doppler compensation, is retained as a parameterized front end wherever it incorporates established physical knowledge, with the learnable encoder built upon it. The extent of this physical integration depends on the task and constitutes a key architectural distinction between the detection and recognition instantiations (Section III-E ). III-C LLM Reasoning Core and Cross-Modal Alignment All input modalities are organized into a single token sequence for the reasoning core, 𝐒 = [ ρ ⁡ ( 𝐙 ac ) ; ψ ⁡ ( 𝐈 env ) ; Emb ⁡ ( 𝐓 ) ] , \mathbf{S}=\big[\,\rho(\mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{ac}});\ \psi(\mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{env}});\ \mathrm{Emb}(\mathbf{T})\,\big], (2) where 𝐙 ac \mathbf{Z}_{\mathrm{ac}} denotes acoustic tokens produced by the encoder, ρ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \rho(\cdot) a lightweight acoustic projection, 𝐈 env \mathbf{I}_{\mathrm{env}} environmental imagery with its encoder ψ \psi , and Emb ⁡ ( 𝐓 ) \mathrm{Emb}(\mathbf{T}) the embeddings of textual tokens including queries, instructions, and physical priors. The concatenated sequence 𝐒 \mathbf{S} is processed by a LLM, which acts as the shared reasoning core: it aggregates evidence across representations and priors, and generates responses in an ordered evidence-then-decision manner. The pretrained parameters are kept frozen and adapted through low-rank modules, so that the pretrained knowledge and instruction-following capability are preserved while the learning burden is confined to the acoustic-to-semantic mapping. Training supervises only the answer portion of each sequence, ℒ = − ∑ i ∈ 𝒜 log P θ ( A i | 𝐒 , A < i ) , \mathcal{L}=-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}}\log P_{\theta}\!\left(A_{i}\,\middle|\,\mathbf{S},A_{<i}\right), (3) where 𝒜 \mathcal{A} denotes the answer token positions, and losses over queries, instructions, and input portions are masked out. This design directs the learning signal toward evidence-based reasoning: extracting relevant evidence from acoustic features and using it to support subsequent decisions, rather than reproducing the input. By sharing a common reasoning core across tasks, the framework also provides a flexible query interface through which a single model addresses vessel identification, physical attribute estimation, and target detection, without relying on separate task-specific output heads. III-D Evidence-Grounded Output The output of the framework is a structured description rather than a bare decision. Formally, each response is organized as 𝒟 = ⟨ d ; e 1 , e 2 , … , e M ⟩ , e m ↔ ℱ m ⊆ ℱ , \mathcal{D}=\langle d;\ e_{1},e_{2},\dots,e_{M}\rangle,\qquad e_{m}\leftrightarrow\mathcal{F}_{m}\subseteq\mathcal{F}, (4) where d d denotes the decision and each evidence item e m e_{m} is linked to a verifiable subset ℱ m \mathcal{F}_{m} of the acoustic features. Evidence statements may refer, for example, to tonal-line locations, modulation patterns, and estimated observation conditions, as well as to missing components that are attributable to the propagation environment rather than to the absence of the target. A fixed field order is imposed on the description, which enforces the evidence-then-decision ordering during generation, renders every attribute individually parseable for evaluation, and allows each reported statement to be independently verified against the input. In this sense, the framework replaces a single opaque score with an inspectable chain whose correctness can be checked link by link. III-E A Unified View of Detection and Recognition Detection and recognition have traditionally been addressed as separate problems using distinct architectures. Within the proposed framework, however, they are two instantiations of a common feature–evidence–decision chain, sharing the same semantic core but differing in four key respects. First, their operating conditions differ. Recognition typically operates on target-bearing segments, often identified through prior detection, where the signal-to-noise ratio is relatively favorable and the relevant physical priors are well established. Detection, by contrast, must operate on continuous observations in which the target, if present, may be deeply buried in noise. Second, their input requirements differ. Recognition primarily processes single-channel, target-centered recordings, whereas detection must accommodate multichannel array signals over extended observation periods and provide spatial information, such as target bearing, alongside target-presence decisions. Third, the extent of physical front-end integration differs. Detection relies more heavily on parameterized physical processing, including array processing, coherent integration, and Doppler compensation, applied before or within the encoder. Recognition, in contrast, places greater emphasis on learning representations of spectral and temporal structure. Fourth, environmental information serves different purposes. In recognition, environmental priors help explain how propagation shapes the observed acoustic features. In detection, they also support predictive modeling of the noise field, fading, and channel-induced distortion. Anticipating these effects is important for identifying potential target signatures against background interference and improving bearing estimation. This paper (Part I) establishes the framework and validates its recognition instantiation, where target-bearing recordings allow the relationship between acoustic features and physical attributes to be examined directly. Part II extends the framework to low-SNR detection, with particular emphasis on multichannel processing and environmental prediction.
